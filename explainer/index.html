In one minuteWhat happens when you type a sentence?

Bhaav-E is a small text-to-video model. You type "a crying emoji bouncing, tears falling, light blue background" and it makes a 2-second looping sticker, 17 frames of 256 × 256 pixels.

Think of a sculptor who starts with a rough block of marble and a written order. At every pass they look at the block, re-read the order, and chip a little away. After 50 passes the sticker is there. The marble is random static, the order is your sentence, and the sculptor is the trained model.

Press ▶ in the bar above to watch it happen. Click any box to open it. Everything on this page was computed in advance, so nothing runs in your browser.

The problemWhy not draw the pixels directly?

One sticker is 17 frames × 256 × 256 pixels × 3 colours ≈ 3.3 million numbers. Asking a model to fix all of them 50 times is slow and wasteful, because most of those numbers describe flat background.

So Bhaav-E works on a summary instead. A borrowed, frozen compressor (the Wan2.2 video VAE) squeezes a clip into 48 × 5 × 16 × 16 = 61,440 numbers, about 54 times smaller. The model only ever edits this summary. The decoder turns it back into pixels once, at the end.

Step 1 · Text EncoderWords become numbers

A computer cannot read "crying". The frozen Flan-T5-Base encoder first cuts the sentence into word pieces it knows: ▁crying stays whole, but bouncing becomes ▁bou + ncing and emoji becomes e + m + oji, then gives each piece a list of 768 numbers that captures its meaning in context. Captions are padded to 35 slots, enough for the longest training caption.

Diffusion Explainer's Stable Diffusion uses CLIP here, a text encoder trained next to an image encoder. T5 never saw an image. It still works because the Refiner learns, through cross-attention, which numbers mean tears and which mean a blue background. Our captions are short and regular, which makes that easy to learn.

Step 2 · RefinerFifty small steps from static to sticker

The Refiner is the only part we trained: a 212M-parameter VideoDiT (a transformer). It starts from pure random noise. At each step it reads three things: the current summary, how noisy it still is (t, from 1 down to 0), and the caption numbers. It answers with a direction v: which way to move to get from static to sticker.

Bhaav-E is trained with rectified flow: during training, noise and clip are joined by a straight line, x_t = (1 − t)·clip + t·noise, and the model learns the line's direction, v = noise − clip. At sampling time we walk back along it in 50 Euler steps: x ← x − v·(t_now − t_next). The steps are not equal: a time shift of 5 spends more of them while t is still high, when the overall shape is decided.

Open the Refiner and watch the middle strip, the model's guess: clip ≈ x − t·v. By step 5 the guess already shows a crying face. The remaining 45 steps sharpen the details and the motion.

GuidanceHow strongly to follow the words

At every step the model is asked twice: once with your caption, once with an empty one. The difference is what the words add. Guidance scales it up: v = v_empty + g · (v_caption − v_empty).

  • g = 0: ignores the words, so you get some sticker, not yours.
  • g = 1: the plain caption prediction.
  • g = 3: Bhaav-E's default.
  • g = 6: pushes harder; colours and effects can get exaggerated.

The orange heat strip in the Refiner shows where the words changed the prediction at each step, for example around the tears.

Step 3 · DecoderFrom summary back to pixels

After step 50 the frozen Wan2.2 decoder expands 61,440 numbers into 17 frames. It undoes the 16× squeeze in width and height and the 4× squeeze in time: latent frame 0 becomes frame 1, and each later latent frame becomes 4 frames. Frame 17 equals frame 1, so the sticker loops.

Decoding takes about 14 seconds per clip on a laptop, so this page fully decodes the guess only at steps 5, 10, 20, 30 and 50 (dots on the slider). Between them it shows a quick look: a simple linear mix of the 48 channels into red, green and blue that we fitted ourselves. It is blurry, but it is the true summary at every step.

CompareChange one phrase, keep the same noise

Each prompt has a twin that differs in one part: the effect, the motion or the background. Press Compare the pair. Both start from the same static. The chart flattens each step's 61,440-number summary to two numbers (a PCA projection of the pair's two paths), so you can see where they split.

Rectified flow trains straight paths, and it shows: two numbers keep about 99% of the variation of 102 summaries, which only happens when both paths are close to straight lines from the shared start to two different endings.

How it learnedTraining runs the same line forwards

Training never runs the 50 steps. For each of 61,571 training clips (already compressed once and saved), it picks a random t, mixes the clip with fresh noise, and asks the model for the direction v = noise − clip. The loss is the squared error of that guess. That is repeated in batches of 64 for 40,000 steps (about 3.6 hours on one GPU).
